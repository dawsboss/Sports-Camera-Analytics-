# First training run: a gaming PC, 94 ball tags, 12 pitch frames

`docs/TRAINING.md` run end to end for the first time. Every number here
comes from a script in this repository run on this machine; the commands
are the ones `docs/TRAINING.md` now gives.

## The machine

Windows 11, RTX 3090 Ti (24 GB, also driving the desktop), i7-12700K,
64 GB RAM, driver 591.86. Native Windows, no WSL: Python 3.13, torch
2.14 + cu130, Ultralytics 8.4.160. `pytest` passes here (75 tests,
~35 s).

## The data, checked before training on it

**Public ball set** (`martinjolif/football-ball-detection`, 1,237 images):
every image 1920×1080 with one ball; box width median 11.7 px, 10th
percentile 8.3, 90th 17.4. Ours is 11. Stage one trains at 1920 with no
scale correction.

**Public pitch set** (317 images): keypoint order matches `VERTICES` at
all 32, by `fetch_public.py`'s `flip_idx` check.

**Our tags** (`spike/labels/tags.json`): 94 ball samples in ten bursts
between 18 and 63 minutes of `20260919-flight`, 91 visible; 12 pitch
frames between 7 and 61 minutes, two of them looking at the right-hand
goal. Held out: 50 minutes to the end (`--holdout-window
20260919-flight=50:`), which is 41 ball samples (40 visible) and 2 pitch
frames. That leaves 53 ball samples and 10 pitch frames to train on. The
green `20260920-future` match has no tags and was never trained on.

### The tags were one frame late about half the time

A contact sheet of every ball tag on the frame `build_dataset.py` cut
showed two fast, zoomed bursts where the box sat beside the ball
instead of on it. OpenCV's seek was not the cause: it returned exactly
the frame ffmpeg's accurate seek did, for six tags checked. Nor was the
file: no edit list offset, no composition offsets, constant 30000/1001
from zero.

The tagger was. It keyed each tag `Math.round(currentTime × 29.97)`, and a
paused video shows the frame whose interval *contains* `currentTime` —
the floor. Past mid-frame the key named the next frame.
`spike/labels/check_tag_timing.py` runs a COCO detector on crops around
each tap at offsets −6 to +3 and asks which frame puts the ball nearest
the tap:

| | tags |
| --- | --- |
| offset −1 best | 15 |
| offset 0 best | 13 |
| offset −2 best | 3 |
| offset −4 best | 1 (the throw-in: the detector found the spare balls on the touchline, not the one overhead) |
| `floor(t × fps)` within 2 px of the best | **29 of 32** |

On the fast bursts that moved the error from 9–18 px at the key to 4–9
px. Fixed on both ends: `build_dataset.py` cuts `floor(t × fps)` for any
ball tag that stores `t` (52 of 94 moved one frame), and the tagger now
keys by the floor at the exact rate. After the fix a few boxes on the
fastest pass are still 10–20 px off — tap precision, or the phone
sometimes showing a frame two back — which a per-tag timing correction
cannot recover without guessing, and is one reason the scoring below
uses distance to the tap with a 20 px radius rather than box overlap.

## Baselines on the held-out tags, before any training

`ball_on_tags.py`: is the most confident detection (at `--accept 0.25`)
within 20 px of the tap?

| | found | wrong place | missed | worst gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| COCO `yolo11x`, class 32, 1920 | 5 / 40 (12%) | 1 | 34 | 12 |
| WASB soccer weights, cold (`wasb_ball.py`) | 0 / 40 | — | — | — |

**The COCO number is far below what `ball_recall.py` reports for this
match** (43% in `docs/NEXT.md`, 63% in this run), and the difference is
partly what each counts. `ball_recall.py`
counts a sample when anything clears the floor; this counts it when the
top detection is on the tapped ball. The held-out stretch is also hard:
twelve samples of a throw-in with the ball overhead against a dark tree
line, a dim passage, the ball among feet.

**WASB is not a shortcut on this footage.** Its released soccer weights
(ISSIA: static 1080p cameras, Serie A) find the ball in 11 of 13 frames
of its own test clip through the same wrapper, which rules out the
wiring, and in 8 of 13 with the frame repeated three times, so on its
home turf the appearance carries most of it. On our held-out tags: 0 of
40 either way, the heatmap at the ball 0.007 where its threshold is 0.5,
and about 8,000 pixels of each 512×288 frame scoring higher. Neither its
appearance model nor its motion cue survives worn turf and a panning
camera. On a *static* camera — the product — the motion half becomes
available again, so it is worth one more run when static footage exists.

## Stage one: the public ball set

`yolo11s`, 1920 px, `batch=6`, `mosaic=0.0 scale=0.2`, 60 epochs, AdamW
0.002 (chosen by `optimizer=auto`). On the public set's own val split
(broadcast frames, not ours):

| epoch | precision | recall | mAP50 | mAP50-95 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.50 | 0.57 | 0.50 | 0.20 |
| 55 (`best.pt`) | 0.96 | 0.90 | 0.95 | 0.60 |
| 60 (`last.pt`) | 0.99 | 0.91 | 0.96 | 0.59 |

That says the recipe learns an 11 px ball on broadcast turf. What it
does on ours is below.

**The first attempt was stopped at epoch 41 for running the machine out
of memory**, with the jobs queued behind it. Likely cause: `cache=ram`
on Windows, where each dataloader worker is a spawned process that can
hold its own copy of the 5.8 GB cache (8 train and 16 val workers), on a
64 GB machine that is also somebody's desktop. Not verified directly.
Resumed from epoch 41 with no cache and four workers: 46 s an epoch
against 40 s cached, and 23 GB of RAM free throughout. Even uncached,
the 15 Python processes of one run hold about 24 GB, because every
spawned worker imports torch. Before any of that, `batch=8` asked for
more than the card's 24 GB and the driver paged 7.5 GB into system RAM
rather than erroring: 1.7 s a batch at a third of rated power, against
0.22 s at `batch=6`.

## Stage two: fine-tuned on 53 tags from one match

From stage one's `best.pt`: AdamW 0.0005, `nbs=6`, no warmup, `mosaic=0.0
scale=0.2`, `patience=20`. Twice: on the 53 training tags, and on those
plus the 25 frames `propagate.py` filled between them. Val is the same
41 held-out tags in both. Both early-stopped (28 and 29 epochs, about two
minutes each). Without propagation the val curve peaked at epoch 8 and
slid (recall 0.51 to 0.30); with it, it held (0.54 at the end).

| COCO `yolo11x` | 5 (12%) | 1 | 12 |
| stage one | 19 (48%) | 0 | 12 |
| stage two, `best.pt` † | 21 (52%) | 1 | 12 |
| stage two, `last.pt` | 10 (25%) | 1 | 12 |
| stage two + propagation, `best.pt` † | 22 (55%) | 0 | 5 |
| stage two + propagation, `last.pt` | 21 (52%) | 2 | 12 |

† picked on these same frames. By burst, for stage one and the

of them half the set: this table says less than it seems to.

**2. Random bursts on the green match**, never trained on
(`ball_recall.py`, six bursts of 40, seed 0). "Found" is anything above
0.25. The match ball there is orange and nothing else counted is, so

## What that says

**Fine-tuning on one match makes a detector for that match.** On the
worn match the fine-tune is the best of the four; on the green one it is
the worst, confidently. The 19 Sept match has yellow and black kits, so
the only white thing in its training frames was the ball, and a white
blob is what 53 frames taught. The held-out window could not show this,
because it is the same match with the same kits: **a time split measures
generalisation within a match, not to the next one.** The green match
exists for exactly this, and it did its job.

**Stage one is a small-ball specialist.** It never draws a box outside
7–15 px, the public set's range, where COCO's hits on the worn match
have a median of 19 px and reach 40, because the follow-cam zooms in on
play. So it wins the distant dim passage (19 of 20 against 5) and misses
most of the zoomed-in match (16% against 63%). The `scale=0.2` that
keeps an 11 px ball from shrinking also keeps a 35 px ball from ever
being seen in training. The follow-cam's range is 6–40 px; the static
camera's should be narrower, which is a reason to keep this recipe for
that camera and widen it for this one.

**The fine-tunes always draw a 22 px box**, because `build_dataset.py`
writes every tag as a 22 px box round the tap. The centre is what
matters downstream, but a zoomed ball 35 px across is labelled as its
middle, which teaches the box head nothing and may cost recall on
exactly the zoomed shots stage one already misses.

**Propagation helps within a match and hurts across them.** It stopped
the fine-tune overfitting (21 against 10 of 40 at the last epoch on
held-out tags) and made its white-blob habit more confident on the green
match (88% found, one on the ball). It multiplies what a match teaches,
including the wrong lesson.

**The ball is not one ball.** The worn match uses at least three —
white with coloured swirls, white with dark panels, yellow spares — and
the green match an orange one. Spare balls on the touchline are real
balls and every detector finds them; telling the ball in play from a
spare is registration's job (on the pitch or not) and the tracker's, not
the detector's.

**Two numbers here disagree with `docs/NEXT.md`**, from the same script,
seed and bursts: COCO finds 63% of worn-match samples where NEXT.md has
43%, and its median ball there is 19 px where NEXT.md has 11. Not
explained here; a different Ultralytics or torch version is one
candidate. The sheets say this run's hits are real balls.

## What to do next, for the ball

1. **Tag the green match too, and every new match but one**, with bursts
   spread over each; hold out by time within each tagged match, and keep
   the next recorded match whole as the never-trained-on test, which is
   the green match's job until then. Different kits and ball colours are
   the training signal the fine-tune lacked. "Not visible" tags on frames full of white shirts
   are the cheapest correction for what it learned.
2. **Give tags a size.** A second tap on the ball's edge, or a pinch, so
   a zoomed ball is labelled as itself. Until then the 22 px box is
   right for a distant ball and wrong for a zoomed one.
3. **Widen `scale` for follow-cam training**, or add zoomed public
   frames, so stage one learns balls at 20–40 px as well as 11.
4. **Keep COCO `yolo11x` as the follow-cam baseline to beat** on random
   bursts of a match nothing was tuned on. Nothing trained here beats it
   there yet.

## The pitch

**Stage one** (`yolo11s-pose`, 1280 px, `batch=12`, 200 epochs, 21
minutes) on the 255 public training images: box mAP50 0.99, keypoint
mAP50 0.81 and still rising at the last epoch (0.69 at 160, 0.73 at
190). Undertrained; at ten minutes per hundred epochs, 400 is cheap.

**Stage two** from it, on our 10 training frames: AdamW 0.0005,
`nbs=10`, no warmup, 200 epochs, three minutes. Flips on, with our
`flip_idx`.

`pitch_on_tags.py` scores a model against tagged vertices: named at
confidence 0.5 or more and within 25 px of the tag is found; more than
60 px off is wrong.

| | frames | tagged vertices | found | wrong | not named |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| stage one, all 12 (it trained on none) | 12 | 133 | 2 (2%) | 3 | 126 (95%) |
| stage two, the 2 held out | 2 | 25 | 0 | 0 | 25 (100%) |
| stage two, its own 10 training frames | 10 | 108 | 30 (28%) | 8 | 53 (49%) |

Lowering stage one's threshold only adds wrong points: at 0.05 it names
55 of the 133, 45 of them more than 60 px off, median 144 px. Its
overlays put keypoints on open grass, the failure the community weights
showed. Stage two did not even learn its own ten frames well. What it
did learn is the halfway line — halfway top, circle top and circle
bottom are right on held-out footage, and they are the vertices tagged
most — and it invents the rest: on a midfield frame at 58 minutes it put
the right penalty box on open grass, and the homography through those
points drew both boxes beside the centre circle.

**`pitch_keypoints.py` said the opposite**: 4+ keypoints and a fitted
homography in 11 of 12 frames of the worn match, and 7 of 12 on the
green one. That is self-consistency, as its docstring says, and the
overlays show those fits are wrong. The tag scorer exists for this.

So the pitch needs what `docs/TRAINING.md` already said and this run
measured: a stage one far larger than 317 broadcast images (the
SoccerNet conversion, 25.5k), and many more tagged frames than twelve,
covering both ends: the two held-out frames are one of each goal, and
the ten training frames show the right goal once.
